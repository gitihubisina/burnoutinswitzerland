What do we mean by exhaustion?

Exhaustion is what chronic stress at work feels like after a while: the demands keep outrunning the energy you have to meet them, and rest no longer restores it. You are drained on Sunday evening, and a good night’s sleep does not fix it.

If that persists, it can develop into burnout. Burnout is not a medical diagnosis. The WHO describes it in its international classification (ICD-11) as an occupational phenomenon: a syndrome that results from chronic stress at work that has not been successfully managed.

Chronic stress at work

High demands, too few resources to cope with them.

Emotional exhaustion

Feeling drained. This is what the survey measures.

Burnout

When exhaustion lasts and the other two dimensions follow.

Burnout has three dimensions: emotional exhaustion, mental distance from the job (negativity, cynicism) and reduced effectiveness. The Swiss Health Survey asks about the first one only. Federal statistics call it a warning signal for a high risk of burnout, but it is not burnout itself.

6.5 bn

Swiss francs lost every year to stress at work, through absence and reduced performance. That is about 1% of Swiss economic output (estimate).

Exhaustion is an early signal, so it is the place to look before burnout has set in, and it is the one dimension a national survey can compare across cantons. People who report it are not diagnosed with burnout. The first two figures come from the Federal Statistical Office, Swiss Health Survey 2022 (BFS Aktuell 2024), employed people aged 15 to 64. The cost figure is an estimate from the Job-Stress-Index of Gesundheitsförderung Schweiz. It covers productivity lost to stress at work, not to exhaustion alone. Counting disability insurance benefits as well, the total cost to society is put at around CHF 10 billion a year. A 2020 report gave a higher productivity estimate of CHF 7.6 billion.

The Swiss baseline

Every five years Switzerland runs a national health survey. In 2022, almost 11,000 working people were asked how their job affects them. This is not a doctor’s opinion and not a burnout diagnosis. It is people reporting how worn out they feel.

Across Switzerland, 20.9 percent reported emotional exhaustion. Women report it more often than men: 23.9 percent against 18.2, a gap of 5.7 points.

Share of working people reporting emotional exhaustion. Dashed line: Swiss average, 20.9%. Source: Swiss Health Survey 2022 (Obsan).

Basel-Stadt: the highest of the three

25.4 percent, one in four, and 4.5 points above the Swiss average. The survey cannot tell us why. But we can look at what working life in Basel is built on.

Basel is a hub for pharma and life sciences. About 200,000 people work in this small city-canton, and about 90,500 commute in every day: 36,000 from abroad and 54,500 from other cantons. High pay usually comes with high demands: responsibility, tight deadlines, being reachable. And work stress is rising across the country: in ten years the share of stressed workers went from 18 to 23 percent. And stress and exhaustion go together: among workers who are stressed most or all of the time, 53 percent also feel emotionally exhausted, against 13 percent of those who feel no stress.

Two cautions. The survey asks people who live in Basel-Stadt, not everyone who works there. And it measures how exhausted people are, not why. These are features of Basel’s working world, not proven causes.

The twist: in Basel the gender pattern flips

26.0 percent of men and 24.7 percent of women report emotional exhaustion. The difference is small, so handle it with care.

One hypothesis, not a findingThe same logic runs through this whole story: exhaustion depends on the total demands people carry. Across Switzerland, men’s exhaustion hardly differs by age or industry, so the industry alone is unlikely to explain Basel. What may matter is how demanding the jobs are: Basel’s well-paid pharma and life-science roles come with high responsibility, and full-time roles are held more often by men, 79 percent of working men against 42 percent of working women. If those demands fall more on men, they could close the usual gap or even reverse it. In Aargau the load sits elsewhere, on women who combine part-time work with most of the unpaid work at home. We cannot test this with our data.

Ticino: the same level from a different working world

Exhaustion is largely a question of balance: how high the demands are and how many resources people have to cope with them. In Basel the demands come from responsibility and performance pressure. In Ticino they look different: the lowest wages in Switzerland and almost 79,000 cross-border workers, roughly three in ten jobs. Different pressures, similar result.

One number. Two realities.

Women are at around 22.3 percent, about the level of women in Ticino. Men are at around 17.0, the lowest of any group we looked at. The gap of more than five points is almost exactly the Swiss gap of 5.7. The low total is two very different realities averaged into one comfortable-looking number.

Why would Aargau’s women carry more? We cannot prove it, but the context points somewhere.

The canton’s own studies describe gaps in childcare: few places and low use. Fewer paid hours do not mean fewer hours of work. For many women it can mean two jobs. This is a hypothesis, not a finding.

The 52% figure is older data (about 2018–19).

Aargau isn’t special. It is the Swiss pattern, visible once you split the average.

Being honest about the data

  1. Our Aargau figures are estimates read from charts. Exact numbers were not available.
  2. All figures are self-reported. If people don’t say they are running on empty, part of the “gender gap” may be a “who says it out loud” gap.
  3. The survey measures how exhausted people are, not why. Our explanations are hypotheses, not results.
  4. Differences of one or two points: handle with care.

So: does your postcode matter?

Less than you’d think, and not in the way you’d think. Between the three cantons the spread is about six percentage points. Inside Aargau alone, between women and men, it is more than five. The gap within a canton is nearly as big as the gap between cantons.

The postcode tells us what kind of working world people live in: a pharma hub like Basel, a low-wage border economy like Ticino, a canton like Aargau where half of all couples split work the traditional way. That is where we would look next.

Next time someone shows you a single national number about how we’re all doing, ask them to split it. That is usually where the truth is sitting.
